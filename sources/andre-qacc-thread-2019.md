## Core arguments from the thread (for opinion.html)

### 1. SPR's 2.5-mile access standard is unreasonable
SPR's *People, Dogs and Parks Strategic Plan* (2014–2017) defends OLA coverage by saying most residents are within 2.5 miles of an OLA. Andre's argument:

> "the Green Lake loop is just under three miles in length and takes about an hour for the average person to walk. So, SPR is basically claiming that a daily walk around Green Lake is a reasonable distance to walk to use a park — but don't forget to double that for the return!"

**Methodology challenge**: TPL's industry-standard park-access metric is a **10-minute walk (0.5 mile)** along the walkable road network. SPR's 2.5-mile standard is 5× longer. At the TPL standard, ~33% of Seattleites have walkable OLA access (our straight-line estimate). At 2.5 miles, nearly everyone is "served." The standard drives the answer.

### 2. Kinnear is a textbook "unreasonable" dog park
- 30–35 minute walk from center of Queen Anne (Trader Joe's reference point)
- Hundreds of stairs cutting through the greenbelt
- Chronic homeless encampments immediately adjacent and on access paths
- ~Two-tennis-court sized fenced area of dirt/bark (dust in summer, mud in winter)
- No water (drinking or hose-off)
- Posted sign advises "exercise no more than 1–2 dogs at a time" — SPR's own signage confirms the capacity problem
- Needles observed; homeless individual reported sleeping inside the dog run
- Women in the neighborhood report feeling unsafe
- Medium/large dogs cannot safely throw a tennis ball without it leaving the park

### 3. Budget allocation is trivially small
0.06% of SPR budget in 2018 ($106K of $168M). Order-of-magnitude below any reasonable proportional-use standard (25% of residents use OLAs per SPR's own survey).

### 4. Enforcement is structurally inadequate
Two animal-control officers covering 6,414 acres. No police power (cannot stop someone who walks away). Fines cap at $150. Most encounters are verbal warnings. Seattle's PRR C049204 data confirms the shape: 4,803 citations 2014–2019, 90%+ were first-offense warnings at $54 or $0.

### 5. The 2014 process produced a non-result
City Council commissioned the plan in 2014. SPR ran a year-plus of surveys and live interviews with dog owners, non-owners, and COLA. Consistent recommendations emerged for shared-use models (time-zoned access, segmented spaces, better enforcement of existing shared spaces) — none were adopted. The "updated" policy added budget for the two patrol officers and little else.

- SPR's *People, Dogs and Parks Strategic Plan*: https://www.seattle.gov/parks/about-us/policies-and-plans/people-dogs-and-parks-strategic-plan
- Seattle Magazine coverage: https://www.seattlemag.com/arts-and-culture/new-leash-master-plan-has-dog-owners-snarling

### 6. Political hook
Andrew Lewis (then running for District 7, elected 2019) was publicly aware of the issue during his campaign. Other council candidates also engaged. Worth checking current council members' positions — many seats have turned over since 2019.

## Potential recommendations to develop

From the thread:
1. **Shared-use models** modeled on NYC and other large cities — morning/evening hours for off-leash in designated parks, with enhanced pickup enforcement.
2. **Time-zoned access** on existing parks rather than building scarce new dedicated OLAs.
3. **Enforcement focus on cleanup and conflict, not absolute leash status** — scarce enforcement resources aimed at the actual nuisance (uncontrolled dogs, feces) rather than compliant but technically illegal off-leash use.
4. **East Queen Anne Park as the next OLA site** — per 2023 QACC park assessment recommendation.
5. **Replace the 2.5-mile SPR access standard with a 10-minute / 0.5-mile walk standard** matching TPL methodology used for Seattle's overall ParkScore.

## Full thread text

### Post 1 — framing and data

Hi,

I appreciated your polite request, and thought I'd take a moment to give a bit of history and context that might help explain why so many dog owners are "off leash scofflaws" in the city of Seattle. The ultra-brief version is that the Seattle Parks and Recreation Department (SPR) has been unable to service the needs of dog owners in the wake of Seattle's boom, leading those owners to run their dogs off leash in parks.

A bit of background data:

- In 2018, the overall 2018 SPR budget was $168,282,650 ($168M); the amount allocated to off leash parks (OLAs) in the same year was $106,000 (or .06% of the budget).
- SPR acreage covers 6,414 acres in 485+ parks. Of that, the OLA system covers 26 acres in 14 parks (or .004% of total SPR land). Seattle in total consists of about 53,113 acres. This means that Seattle Parks take up about 12% of Seattle's total acreage — but OLAs make up just .00048% of city land.
- In 1980, Seattle's under-18 population stood at 87,000. Thirty years later, in 2010, it had only inched up to 95,000. And for the first half of the current decade, that number remained basically flat, even as the city grew at a record-breaking pace. Then, from 2015 to 2017, the under-18 population shot up to 115,000. And SPR surveys show that dog owners represent about 25-30% of the city's population, and 25% of the city's population uses off-leash areas.
- Census Data in 2021 found Seattle has more dogs (153,000) than children (107,000). And that number has been on the rise for the last decade.

Those are the hard facts. But even anecdotally, it's clear that there's nowhere for dog owners to run their dogs in a reasonably sized, safe, open area that's within easy access to homes and neighborhoods. And that has led people to ignore the rules since no reasonable alternative has been given.

I recognize that some are already ramping up arguments, including "it's against the law!", or "look up the dog parks and go there", or even in Queen Anne "go to the dog park in Kinnear park!" In an attempt to be brief here, I'll just say there is plenty more data and/or counter-arguments (which I'm happy to share in polite conversation here if asked) to explain why so many people ignore SMC 18.12.080 (the law in question). I'd encourage those of you who disagree to first read the complete study SPR did at the city council's request in 2014:

https://www.seattle.gov/parks/about-us/policies-and-plans/people-dogs-and-parks-strategic-plan

… and I will warn you that the results of SPR's investigation here are controversial. I don't think it's unfair to say SPR basically punted (on what is a very hard problem), and continued the same failing process and system — even after many warnings by participants of that process. I've personally spoken to several of the folks running to represent Queen Anne in the city council and shared this information (and much more) in the hopes that we can revisit and find a solution (perhaps shared parks with certain morning/evening hours for pets?) that works better. But until that changes, I'd politely suggest to you that things aren't going to change — simply because there's no alternative.

Final note: none of the above is intended to excuse "bad" dog owners who don't control their dogs, can't keep them away from children's play areas, don't pick up after their animal, etc. While there's a real reason people ignore SMC 18.12.080, there is no excuse to not control and pick up after your animal.

### Post 2 — Kinnear-specific

Here's a bit of information on Kinnear: https://www.seattle.gov/parks/find/parks/kinnear-park. That said, it's a good example of an "unreasonable" dog park. Assuming you are starting roughly in the center of Queen Anne (say the Trader Joe's), it's about a 30-35 minute walk to get to the park. When you get there you have to go down hundreds of stairs cutting through the greenbelt, with poor lines of sight and current and abandoned homeless encampments to the left and right (along with associated debris). When you finally get to the actual dog park, you find a roughly two-tennis court sized fenced in area of dirt and a bit of bark — a dustpit in summer, a mudpit in winter. There is no water for the dogs, either to drink or even a concrete pad to hose them off from the mud puddle. And to add insult to injury, there are several homeless encampments right *next* to the dog park, including one literally blocking (as in you can't open the gate) one of the two entrances to it. I have personally seen discarded needles in the area, and one person reported finding a homeless person sleeping something off *inside* of the actual dog run. I don't feel comfortable going to this park, much less several women I have spoken to. It's very isolated — other than the homeless who live in the greenbelt. It's also too small — there are signs posted on the dog park billboard to not "exercise" more than 1-2 dogs at a time in the park because they have no room to run or have space and avoid possible negative interactions. Anyone with a medium or larger dog who tries to throw a tennis ball literally has to be careful to not accidentally throw it outside of the park.

Finally, to return to the distance of the park from homes: SPR's report attempts to spin dog park support as "good" because there is a dog park within two and a half miles of most residents. But pause and think about what that *means*: maybe half (or more, in areas like Queen Anne) don't have reasonable walking access to a dog park. I put myself in the mindset of a parent of either kids or dogs who comes home in the evening after work. I'm looking for a park within maybe a 10 minute walk — not two plus miles away! (For reference, the Green Lake loop is just under three miles in length and takes about an hour for the average person to walk. So, SPR is basically claiming that a daily walk around Green Lake is a reasonable distance to walk to use a park — but don't forget to double that for the return!)

### Post 3 — pragmatic summary

Short, pragmatic, and hopefully fair answer: due to a lack of access to reasonable (defined both by size and easy walkability) dog parks in Seattle (in general) and Queen Anne (specifically), yes, many of the parks in the city are used by dog owners who run them off-leash. It's technically illegal, but enforcement is (literally) two dudes who are tasked with driving around 50,000+ acres of Seattle parkland and warning people who are letting their dogs run off-leash that they shouldn't do it. Technically after a few warnings they can give you a $150 maximum fine. But since they aren't police, they can't actually stop you if you walk away (unless, of course, they have a police officer with them, which just isn't going to happen right now). So, the rule is an often ignored joke.

Pragmatically, nothing is going to change anytime soon since the city is unable to increase enforcement or fines (as it would be regressive), can't make new parks (due to lack of land and funds), and dog owners have nothing to lose. I have suggested multiple times that the QACC, City, and Parks Department look at a shared park use model where dog owners can use the park certain hours and enforcement is more focused on making sure that the park is protected and kept clean for everyone's use. But that also doesn't seem to be in the cards.

For context, I've been in Seattle using the parks (respectfully, when kids aren't playing) with my dogs for 20+ years and have seen the enforcement people *once*. Odds are in your favor — especially since you can just walk away politely if need be.

### Post 4 — history of the 2014 process

Seeing as this thread has become a pretty good collection of information and status on the off-leash/lack of dog park support in Seattle issue, I thought I would share a bit of history of where the Animal Control patrol program came from, and why it's not working.

As I mentioned above, in 2014 the city council asked Seattle Parks and Rec to review the issue and come up with a new policy around the issue of dogs and park use. That led SPR to doing over a year of online questionnaires and surveys, as well as in-person interviews with groups of both dog owners and parents with children. Many people were involved with these discussions, including great groups like COLA, and I was lucky enough to join one of the live interview groups.

Although it's impossible to summarize all of the feedback here, anyone who wants to can read the full report at the SPR link above.

A few key points were mentioned over and over to SPR:

1. There aren't enough dog parks to support demand, and that's leading to tensions between dog and non-dog owners.
2. Everyone on all sides agreed that the concerns of non-dog fans were completely valid — there is no reason for non-dog people (however you choose to define them) to have to deal with dogs in their faces, feeling threatened, or dealing with feces.
3. However, everyone also agreed that the concerns of dog owners were just as valid, and that there weren't realistic options to make more, better, large dog parks (Seattle land scarce, expensive, etc).
4. That generally led to recommendations that Seattle adopt shared dog park policies similar to New York and other large cities. Possibilities included shared use with better poop patrol/enforcement, park use zoned by hours (i.e. mornings/evenings dogs allowed), or segregating portions of the parks (whether by solid physical barrier, or subtle lines that depend on dog owner management of their animals).
5. Finally, the point was made over and over again that the current (pre-2014) policy had failed and was unworkable. There weren't enough animal control officers to make a dent in patrolling over 6,400 acres of park land, and even if they happened across someone they generally give warnings. And on the off chance it was a repeated offense, the dog owner *might* get a $150 fine. This last point is key — many people stated that the current policy was broken, was not working, and had to change.

The hope after all of this outreach was that we would land on something more balanced. Unfortunately, that is where SPR failed. The "updated" policy literally changed almost nothing. A little bit of budget was added to make the two animal patrol officers full-time, but the big changes (particularly changing policy to allow shared use of limited park resources in some manner) were ignored with vague responses of "costs too much" or being hard to enforce (just as current rules are).

https://www.seattlemag.com/arts-and-culture/new-leash-master-plan-has-dog-owners-snarling

So that's where we are today. A failed policy continues to be the rule of the land, and is basically ignored by all. The animal patrol can't actually shift behavior with current resources and tools, and the odds of even being caught are incredibly slim.

One positive note: some folks running for City Council, including Andrew Lewis, are aware of the problem and think SPR needs to revisit this problem.
