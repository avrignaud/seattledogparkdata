Part I · The Gap

Seventeen years of standing still, while the city grew.

Seattle has added one net off-leash area since 2009 — while its population grew 34% and the rest of the parks system has tripled in budget. The math hasn't been moving in dogs' favor, and the per-capita gap against peer cities has widened.

Andre Vrignaud · Seattle Dog Park Data · Updated April 23, 2026

Finding 01Residents per off-leash area, over time.

In 2010 Seattle had 14 OLAs serving 609K residents — about 43,500 people per off-leash area. Fifteen years later the OLA count is still 14, and the city has added 207K residents. The ratio has moved 34% in the wrong direction.

Seattle residents per OLA, 2010–2026
One net new OLA opens in fall 2026 (West Seattle Stadium). The ratio drops — but only to where it sat in 2013.
Source: U.S. Census annual estimates + SPR OLA count. seattle-timeseries.csv
Observation

From 2010 to 2025, Seattle added roughly 207,000 residents and zero net off-leash areas. The 2026 opening brings the per-OLA ratio back to 2013 levels — which is to say, a decade behind the population curve.

Finding 02The peer-city comparison.

Against eleven other US and Canadian cities, Seattle sits near the bottom of the per-capita OLA rankings. Portland has 3.2× Seattle's per-capita supply. Boise has 4.2×. Even with the 2026 opening, Seattle's ratio sits below all but five of the comparison cities.

Fully-fenced OLAs per 100,000 residents
Seattle counts only fully-fenced OLAs. Portland, SF, and Vancouver BC figures normalized to the same definition where possible — see methodology.
Source: city parks departments, 2024–2025. peer-cities.csv · methodology

The methodology caveats are meaningful here. Portland counts some unfenced voice-control areas that Seattle would exclude; Vancouver BC includes time-restricted shared-use beach and field access. The peer-cities page walks through each city's counting rules individually — normalization narrows the gap but does not close it.

Observation

Adjusted for definitional differences, Seattle still sits in the bottom third of peer-city per-capita OLA supply. The gap is not created by counting methodology.
