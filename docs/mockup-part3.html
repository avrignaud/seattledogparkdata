Section 3aWhat works: the three OLAs people actually love.

Not all 14 OLAs are equal. Three consistently show up at the top of resident feedback (SPR 2023 Expansion Study open-ended responses, Nextdoor mentions, Google reviews): Magnuson, Genesee, and Marymoor. They are not the closest-to-downtown, the newest, or the most expensive. They share three design attributes.

9.0 ac

Scale

All three exceed 2 fenced acres. Large enough that multiple dogs can move, sort themselves, and disengage. The small-OLA failure mode — fights, corner-cornering — doesn't appear.

100%

Dedicated entrance

Each has a marked OLA entry separate from main park paths. Conflict rates with non-dog park users drop sharply when shared gates are eliminated.

3/3

Water & surface

Working water stations, and a surface that drains. Seattle's rain plus turf equals mud; the three well-regarded OLAs all have woodchip or gravel surface treatments.

The pattern in the liked OLAs is not "big", "new", or "expensive." It's "room to move, a clear gate, and a surface that drains after rain."

— Observed pattern, Expansion Study open-ended responses

Section 3bShared-use: the off-leash-hours model.

Six peer cities — New York, Vancouver BC, Boise, Washington DC, Minneapolis, and Arlington VA — designate specific parks as off-leash during specific hours (typically 9pm–9am), rather than building dedicated fenced OLAs. The rule is enforced by dogs being on-leash the rest of the day.

This is strictly cheaper than building. NYC has operated off-leash hours since 1986 at roughly 80 parks, at zero incremental capital cost beyond signage. Complaint volumes per off-leash-hour park in NYC run lower than Seattle's per-park complaint rates today — because the model legitimizes the use instead of treating it as violation.

New York · since 1986

9pm–9am

Off-leash hours at ~80 parks citywide. Formalized in 2007 after a decade of informal tolerance. Parks closed for the night hours; enforcement redirected to daytime on-leash compliance.

Washington DC

Dawn & dusk

15+ "dog exercise areas" designated within existing parks. Mixed fenced and unfenced; off-leash permitted only during posted windows.

Minneapolis

Seasonal

Off-leash hours at some parks run only outside peak summer months. An example of shared-use that explicitly yields to other park users during high-demand periods.

Section 3cNon-SPR land: public space SPR doesn't manage.

Seattle owns a lot of public space that isn't an SPR park. Port-owned land, SDOT right-of-way wedges, SPU reservoir buffers, school playfields after hours. Most of it sits unused outside narrow windows. None of it appears in the OLA inventory because only SPR lands do.

Port of Seattle

Waterfront parcels

Specific underused Port parcels on Elliott and Duwamish. Port has shown willingness to partner on public access (Jack Block, Smith Cove). The operating model is a lease-to-SPR, not a Port build.

SDOT right-of-way

Residual ROW wedges

Intersection remnants, greenway-adjacent leftover land. SDOT has precedent for converting these to people-space (parklets, Stay Healthy Streets). Dog-use is a small additional stretch.

Seattle Public Schools

Fenced playfields, after hours

Joint-use agreements already exist (public community use of fields). Adding after-hours off-leash windows costs zero capital and uses already-fenced space. Requires SPS partnership.

Part III · Closing

What we're asking for.

The point of this report isn't to pick a winner between the three options. It's to get any of them off the whiteboard. Concretely, three near-term items that don't require a new capital cycle:

  • 01SPR pilots off-leash hours at three non-OLA parks for 12 months, with enforcement redirected to clean-park compliance.
  • 02SPR publishes the OLA-only share of the "Maintaining Parks & Facilities" BSL annually, so the community can track actual OLA spend.
  • 03City establishes an inter-departmental task force (SPR + SDOT + SPU + SPS + Port) to inventory non-SPR public land suitable for shared off-leash use.

None require new money. All three are enforceable by existing policy tools. The ask is attention, not budget.
