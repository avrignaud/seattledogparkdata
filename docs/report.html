What this report does

The site seattledogparkdata.com reopens the specific claims SPR makes about off-leash access, checks each against primary sources, and publishes the underlying data. This document is the condensed print/PDF edition of that site, optimized for a single read-through.

Every number in this report links to its source. The methodology and the scripts that compute derived figures (network walkshed, enforcement aggregates, per-dog spending) are in the public GitHub repository.

What this report does not do

It does not argue that dog owners have a constitutional right to off-leash space in every park. It does not argue that Seattle should spend on OLAs in proportion to dog ownership. It does not argue that individual rule-breaking is justified. It argues — on the opinion page, clearly marked as such — that the current policy has failed at the system level and that a shared-use / time-zoned alternative used for 20+ years in New York City is worth piloting.

Section 02 · Part I

The Gap — Seattle has added one dog park in fifteen years.

The city's population grew by 200,000. Its parks budget nearly tripled. The first net new OLA since 2009 opens in late 2026. This is the supply problem, with sources.

Residents per OLA, 2010–2026
Simple division: population ÷ OLA count. 43,500 in 2010 → 58,300 in 2025 → back to 52,000 in 2026 with the two new openings. Still worse than 2010.
Source: WA OFM April 1 estimates · SPR OLA inventory
Dog parks per 100,000 residents — West Coast peers + benchmarks
Seattle sits at 1.82, next to Austin (which ranks 54th out of 100 on TPL's overall ParkScore). West Coast peers cluster around 5.
Source: Trust for Public Land 2025 ParkScore · Vancouver Park Board
What this section argues: Seattle's OLA supply has been frozen at 14 for roughly fifteen years while the population grew 34%. On a per-capita basis, Seattle runs at roughly one-third the density of Portland, San Francisco, or Vancouver BC. The two new OLAs opening in fall 2026 — the first net increase since 2009 — do not close the per-capita gap, or even return Seattle to its own 2010 ratio. Full data in data/seattle-timeseries.csv.

Section 03 · Part II

Access — 99% live near a park. 9.6% live near a dog park.

The walkability paradox: Seattle's park system is a national benchmark; its off-leash system is a dense-urban failure. The difference comes down to whether you have a dog.

Where the system is undersized, by amenity

Seven of 14 OLAs are below the AKC 1-acre minimum. Four are below a quarter-acre (pocket-run size). COLA's user survey adds the amenity picture:

  • Only 3 of 14 OLAs have lighting (Plymouth Pillars, Regrade, Golden Gardens). Fall and winter evenings after work are unusable at the other 11.
  • Only 1 of 14 has water access (Magnuson).
  • Only 4 of 14 have small-dog areas.
  • 78% of total OLA acreage is in 4 parks: Magnuson, Westcrest, Genesee, Dr. Jose Rizal. The other 10 share the remaining 22% — which is why the SPR 2016 survey's #1 reason non-OLA users don't use OLAs is "not conveniently located."
The Kinnear case study. Queen Anne's only OLA is 0.1 acre (about two tennis courts), at the bottom of a hillside greenbelt with a 20-year documented history of chronic homeless encampments. A neighbor, quoted from the 2021 Nextdoor thread: "Have you been to the only official dog park on Queen Anne? It's in Lower Kinnear Park. Not visible from the street, next to the tents full of addicts and severely mentally challenged men. I am not going to risk my life for it. My dog stays on leash."

Section 04 · Enforcement

4,803 off-leash citations. Where they happened, and where they didn't.

Seattle Animal Control's own records (public records request C049204, 2014–2019) show where dog owners get cited. The pattern is simple: citations cluster where OLAs aren't, or where the one that exists is too small to matter.

Top 20 cited parks, 2014–2019
Red bars = no designated OLA. Green = OLA host. Amber = large park with small partial OLA.
Source: data/enforcement-by-park-year.csv · raw files in data/prr-responses/C049204/
Six of the top ten cited parks have no designated OLA at all. Discovery Park alone accounts for 9% of all citations in the dataset. Enforcement tripled from 2015 (519) to 2018 (1,276) — the "Making Parks Safer" program doing its job on the output side. But 90% of citations are first-offense warnings at $0 or $54. Against a dog population of at least 150,000, that is roughly a 0.5% annual enforcement probability per dog. Citations are not changing behavior; they are logging it. A follow-up public records request for October 2019 to present has been filed. The signed 2021 MOA pins the FAS-side cost of the current staffing model at $152,399 per officer per year for roughly 160 officer-hours per month covering 485+ parks.

Section 05 · Budget Deep Dive

It is a money problem — but not the one you'd think.

Seattle's SPR budget is one of the best-funded municipal park systems in the country. Its dedicated OLA line is a rounding error. The gap is in allocation, not total spend.

Seattle SPR and related spending lines, 2026 proposed — one axis, log scale
On a linear axis the OLA-only bar ($129K) is literally invisible next to SPR total ($507M). Log scale is the only way they fit together. The OLA-only line is 3,929× smaller than the SPR total.
Source: Seattle 2025-26 SPR Proposed Budget · Seattle 2026-2031 Proposed CIP · Parkways · data in data/budget-detail.csv

The peer-city transparency problem

Seattle is, ironically, more transparent about OLA-specific spending than any of its peer cities. Portland, San Francisco, Vancouver BC, Austin, DC, and Minneapolis do not publish dog-park-specific budget lines in their public budget documents as of April 2026. Which means the dollar-per-capita comparison across cities cannot be done without a batch of public records requests to each city's parks department. That is itself the finding — see data/peer-cities-budget.csv.

Section 06 · Opinion & Recommendation

Seattle will not build its way out of this. It needs a policy change.

The rest of this report is factual. This section is opinion. Signed, sourced, and written after 20+ years of walking Seattle's parks with dogs.

Six principles

P1Seattle will never build its way out of the OLA shortage.

The city has added one net dog park in 15 years while the population grew 34%. Even with every site SPR has on the books eventually built, the per-capita gap to Portland, SF, and Vancouver BC doesn't close. The constraint is physical.

P2The little buildable land Seattle has should not go to dog parks first.

Seattle is in a housing-affordability crisis and an ongoing homelessness emergency. I own dogs and I still believe this. Any serious plan starts from the assumption that we will not get more than a handful of new OLAs over the coming decade.

P3Seattle has more dogs than children, and the investment gap reflects that poorly.

SPR's own 2016 survey: 25% of residents use OLAs. The OLA-only budget line was 0.06% of SPR spending in 2018 and smaller every year since. Proportional investment would look nothing like the current allocation.

P4People who don't want to be around dogs have a right not to be.

This is non-negotiable. The status quo — 40% of owners self-reporting monthly-plus illegal off-leash use in general parks — fails these residents directly.

P5Children have a right to play on clean, safe fields.

A playfield with dog feces on it, or a softball practice interrupted by a loose dog, is a system failure. The current policy does not deliver clean playfields — enforcement is post-hoc and structurally insufficient.

P6Families and non-dog-owners deserve reliably dog-free park time.

Access goes both ways. Today, dog owners have tiny, often-unsafe OLAs; non-owners have a city full of parks that are technically dog-free but in practice aren't. Both groups lose.

Three opinions that fall out of the data

O1The current rules have failed, and Seattle is doubling down on the part that isn't working.

The 2014–2017 SPR process heard consistent calls for shared-use / time-zoned models. SPR kept the same framework and added more enforcement. In April 2026, Axios Seattle reported another enforcement expansion. Same lever, pulled harder; at roughly $700K–$1M per year all-in, at ~0.5% annual contact probability per dog.

O2Fines will never work — not at any amount Seattle could plausibly adopt.

4,803 citations over 6 years. 90% first-offense warnings at $0 or $54. Maximum fine: $162. At that schedule, wealthy owners treat citations as a parking ticket; poorer owners are hit regressively. There is no fine-schedule version of this that fixes the structural mismatch.

O3Dog owners will keep running dogs off-leash under the current rules — for rational reasons.

Most neighborhoods have no walkable OLA. Where an OLA exists it is often too small to use safely, or in an unsafe location. Probability of citation is under 1% per year. This is a supply failure producing a compliance failure.

A time-zoned shared-use policy for Seattle's parks.

Adopt a shared-use model similar to New York City's 20-year-running off-leash hours policy. Pair it with a fundamentally different enforcement posture.

  1. Designate shared-use parks by neighborhood — not every park participates. Parks with heavy children's programming stay leashed 24/7.
  2. Time-zone access: off-leash from park-opening to ~9 AM and ~9 PM to close, matching NYC. Pilot and adjust per park.
  3. Redeploy enforcement: the ~$700K–$1M per year currently headed into the expanded ACO program would fund on-site compliance staff at transition hours and post-session cleanup crews.
  4. Fund through expanded dog-license fees, not fine increases. Dedicated to the Seattle Animal Shelter and a clean-park crew.
  5. Keep the small dedicated OLAs as all-day options. They stop being the entire answer.
  6. Measure and publish outcomes — citations, Find-It-Fix-It complaints, post-session cleanliness audits. Pull pilots that aren't working; expand the ones that are.

Honest counterarguments, taken seriously

The full list of seven steel-manned counterarguments — dogs-as-privilege, playfield-harm, budget-ratio-logic, land-is-housing, and more — is in the opinion page on the site. The short version: these arguments are real, some are correct, and none of them is an endorsement of the current status quo. The recommendation above is designed to address every one of them without pretending they don't exist.

— Andre Vrignaud Queen Anne, Seattle · April 2026

Section 07 · Methodology & Sources

Show your work.

Every derived number in this report is reproducible from primary source data using scripts in the public GitHub repository. The key inputs and pipelines are listed here; the full detail is in METHODOLOGY.md.

Primary inputs

  • SPR OLA inventory — seattle.gov, cross-checked with COLA. Stored as data/seattle-olas.csv.
  • Off-leash citation records — Seattle public records request C049204 (filed 2019-08-29, produced 2019-10-15). 4,803 citations, all offense levels, 2014–2019. Raw XLSX files in data/prr-responses/C049204/, consolidated CSV at data/enforcement-citations.csv, built by scripts/build_enforcement_datasets.py.
  • ACO staffing contract — Signed 2021 MOA between SPR and FAS (reference AG21-PRF03-032), full PDF in data/moas/.
  • Population — Washington OFM April 1 official estimates and 2020 Census Decennial P1.
  • Peer-city comparison — Trust for Public Land 2025 ParkScore Index city PDFs; Vancouver BC from its People, Parks & Dogs Strategy.
  • Budget — Seattle City Budget Office archives, Seattle Park District financial plans, Parkways blog reporting.

Derived outputs (reproducible scripts in /scripts)

  • Network walkshed — osmnx against OpenStreetMap, projected to UTM 10N, convex-hull isochrones at 0.5 / 1.0 / 2.5 miles per OLA. Script: scripts/compute_walkshed.py. Output: data/walkshed/ola_isochrones.geojson.
  • Population-weighted coverage — intersection of walkshed unions with TIGER 2020 block groups clipped to Seattle city boundary, Census-population-attributed by area. Script: scripts/population_coverage.py. Output: data/walkshed/population_coverage.csv.
  • Enforcement aggregates — park-name canonicalization regex in scripts/build_enforcement_datasets.py; full data-quality breakdown (83.7% spatially attributable) in data/enforcement-citations.csv.

Data gaps

The site flags where data is missing, approximate, or stale. The most significant open items: (1) post-October-2019 enforcement data (PRR extension filed); (2) FIFI dog-complaint trend (PRR drafted); (3) OLA-only budget share 2025–2026 (PRR drafted); (4) methodology behind SPR's 2.5-mile standard (PRR drafted); (5) per-OLA usage headcounts (SPR may not track; COLA outreach drafted). All draft PRRs are in the /prrs directory of the repo.
